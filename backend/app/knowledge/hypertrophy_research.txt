Hypertrophy is the increase in muscle fiber size through training stimulus.
Two types: Myofibrillar hypertrophy (strength focused) and Sarcoplasmic hypertrophy (size focused).

Optimal rep ranges for hypertrophy according to research:
- 6-12 reps per set is the classic hypertrophy range.
- Research by Schoenfeld (2017) shows muscle growth occurs across a wide rep range (5-30 reps).
- What matters most is training close to failure, not the specific rep range.

Volume requirements for hypertrophy (Dr. Mike Israetel - Renaissance Periodization):
- Minimum Effective Volume (MEV): 10 sets per muscle group per week.
- Maximum Adaptive Volume (MAV): 15-20 sets per muscle group per week.
- Maximum Recoverable Volume (MRV): Beyond 20 sets, recovery becomes impossible.

Frequency: Each muscle group should be trained 2x per week for optimal hypertrophy.
Push Pull Legs 6 days hits each muscle 2x per week - optimal.
Upper Lower 4 days hits each muscle 2x per week - also optimal.
Full Body 3 days hits each muscle 3x per week - good for beginners.

Muscle protein synthesis (MPS) peaks 24-48 hours after training and returns to baseline by 72 hours.
Training a muscle every 48-72 hours is therefore theoretically optimal.

Rest periods for hypertrophy: 60-90 seconds between sets is sufficient.
Longer rest (2-3 minutes) allows more weight to be used, which can also drive hypertrophy.

Time under tension: Each rep should take 2-3 seconds on the way down (eccentric phase).
Controlled eccentric increases muscle damage and hypertrophy stimulus.

Compound movements vs isolation:
- Compound movements (bench press, squat, deadlift) should form the foundation.
- Isolation movements (flyes, curls, lateral raises) add additional volume to specific muscles.
- Ratio: 70% compound, 30% isolation is a good guideline.

Dr. Brad Schoenfeld is widely considered the leading researcher in hypertrophy science.
His 2010 paper on the mechanisms of muscle hypertrophy identified three primary drivers: mechanical tension, metabolic stress, and muscle damage.
Subsequent research has shown mechanical tension to be the most important of the three.

Renaissance Periodization founded by Dr. Mike Israetel introduced the concept of volume landmarks.
Minimum Effective Volume is the least amount of weekly sets that produces measurable muscle growth.
Maximum Adaptive Volume is the optimal range where muscle growth is maximized.
Maximum Recoverable Volume is the point beyond which more training produces diminishing returns or injury.

For most muscle groups the MEV is 10 sets per week, MAV is 15 to 20 sets, and MRV is beyond 20 sets.
Individual variation is significant and these numbers shift based on training age, recovery, and genetics.
Beginners can grow with as few as 5 sets per muscle per week because everything is a new stimulus.

Proximity to failure is the most important variable in hypertrophy training according to recent research.
Sets taken within 3 to 4 reps of failure produce significantly more hypertrophy than sets stopped far from failure.
This does not mean every set must go to absolute failure which increases injury risk and recovery demand.
Leaving 2 reps in reserve on most sets and occasionally going to failure is the evidence-based approach.

Muscle protein synthesis is the biological process by which muscle fibers are repaired and grown.
It is elevated for 24 to 48 hours after a training session and returns to baseline by 72 hours.
Training a muscle group every 48 to 72 hours is therefore theoretically optimal for maximizing growth.
Push pull legs 6 days per week and upper lower 4 days per week both achieve this frequency.

Eccentric training refers to the lengthening phase of a muscle contraction.
Research consistently shows that slow controlled eccentrics of 2 to 4 seconds produce greater hypertrophy than fast eccentrics.
This is because the muscle is under tension for longer and experiences more mechanical damage during the stretch.

Compound movements should form the foundation of any hypertrophy program.
The bench press, squat, deadlift, and overhead press recruit the most total muscle mass.
Isolation movements like curls, flyes, and lateral raises provide additional volume to specific muscles.
A ratio of 70 percent compound to 30 percent isolation is a commonly recommended guideline.

Mind muscle connection refers to consciously focusing on the target muscle during an exercise.
Research by Schoenfeld and Contreras (2016) showed that this focus increases muscle activation in the target area.
For isolation exercises like bicep curls the mind muscle connection is particularly important.
For compound exercises like squats focusing on technique rather than specific muscles is more effective.
