Protein is the most important macronutrient for muscle building and recovery.
Protein provides amino acids which are the building blocks of muscle tissue.

Protein requirements based on research:
- General health: 0.8g per kg bodyweight.
- Muscle building: 1.6-2.2g per kg bodyweight.
- Fat loss while preserving muscle: 2.2-3.0g per kg bodyweight.
- Optimal upper limit: 2.2g per kg for most people. Beyond this shows diminishing returns.

Protein timing:
- Total daily protein matters more than timing according to most research.
- However, consuming 20-40g protein within 2 hours post-workout is beneficial.
- Spreading protein across 4-5 meals maximizes muscle protein synthesis throughout the day.
- Minimum threshold per meal: 20g protein to trigger muscle protein synthesis.

Best protein sources:
- Chicken breast: 31g protein per 100g, low fat, complete amino acid profile.
- Eggs: 13g protein per 100g, highest biological value of any food.
- Paneer: 18g protein per 100g, good for vegetarians, contains casein (slow digesting).
- Dal (lentils): 9g protein per 100g, incomplete protein, combine with rice for complete profile.
- Curd (Greek yogurt): 11g protein per 100g, contains probiotics for gut health.
- Whey protein: 24g protein per scoop, fastest absorbing, ideal post-workout.

Carbohydrates for training:
- Primary fuel source for high intensity training.
- Consume carbs before training for energy and after training for glycogen replenishment.
- Brown rice, oats, sweet potato are superior to white rice due to slower digestion.
- However white rice is not bad - it is easy to digest and works well post-workout.

Calorie targets:
- Muscle gain (bulk): Maintenance calories + 200-400 kcal surplus.
- Fat loss (cut): Maintenance calories - 300-500 kcal deficit.
- Recomposition: Maintenance calories with high protein intake.

Fat intake:
- Minimum 0.5g per kg bodyweight for hormone production.
- Healthy fats: Nuts, olive oil, avocado, fish.
- Do not drop fats below 20% of total calories.

Protein is composed of amino acids which are the building blocks of muscle tissue.
There are 20 amino acids in total of which 9 are essential meaning the body cannot produce them and they must come from food.
Complete proteins contain all 9 essential amino acids and include animal sources like meat, eggs, and dairy.
Incomplete proteins are missing one or more essential amino acids and include most plant sources.

The leucine threshold is the minimum amount of leucine needed to trigger muscle protein synthesis.
Research suggests 2 to 3 grams of leucine per meal is required to maximally stimulate MPS.
A 30 to 40 gram serving of high quality protein typically provides sufficient leucine.
This is why spreading protein across 4 to 5 meals is superior to consuming the same amount in 1 or 2 meals.

Carbohydrates are the primary fuel source for high intensity resistance training.
Muscle glycogen, which is stored carbohydrate in muscle tissue, is the main energy substrate during heavy lifting.
Consuming carbohydrates before training ensures glycogen stores are topped up for optimal performance.
Post workout carbohydrates replenish depleted glycogen stores and support recovery.

The anabolic window is the period after training when the body is most receptive to nutrients.
Early research suggested this window was 30 minutes but more recent research shows it is 2 to 3 hours.
Consuming protein within 2 hours of training is recommended but strict timing is less important than daily totals.

Dietary fat is essential for hormone production including testosterone and growth hormone.
Dropping fat intake below 0.5 grams per kg bodyweight can suppress testosterone and impair muscle growth.
Healthy fat sources include nuts, olive oil, fatty fish, and avocado.
Saturated fat from animal sources is acceptable in moderate amounts as part of a balanced diet.

Hydration directly impacts training performance.
Even mild dehydration of 2 percent of bodyweight reduces strength output by 5 to 10 percent.
Aim for at least 35 ml of water per kg bodyweight daily and increase this during intense training.
Electrolytes including sodium, potassium, and magnesium are lost through sweat and should be replenished.

Creatine monohydrate is the most researched supplement in sports nutrition.
It works by increasing phosphocreatine stores in muscle which provides rapid energy for short intense efforts.
Research consistently shows 3 to 5 grams daily increases strength by 5 to 15 percent over time.
It is safe, cheap, and effective with no significant side effects reported in healthy individuals.

Caloric surplus for muscle gain should be moderate, not aggressive.
A surplus of 200 to 300 calories above maintenance allows muscle growth while minimizing fat gain.
Aggressive bulking with a 500 to 1000 calorie surplus leads to excessive fat accumulation.
The rate of muscle gain for natural lifters is limited to approximately 1 to 2 kg per month at best.
